## 1.0 The Foundation: 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' Business Model and Legal Structure

This section establishes the foundational commercial and legal viability of 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' (RSC). It analyses the target market on Minjerribah (North Stradbroke Island), defines the specific service offerings based on identified market "pain points," and provides a comparative analysis of the legal structures to be presented during consultations with the Straddie Chamber of Commerce.

## 1.1 Foundational Legal Structure: A Comparative Analysis for Chamber Consultation

The initial query notes a pending decision between a for-profit and a co-operative model, contingent on consultation with the Straddie Chamber of Commerce. This analysis provides the strategic comparison for that consultation. The choice of legal structure is the central strategic decision for market entry, as it defines the relationship between RSC and the local business community it intends to serve.

## 1.1.1 Option 1: For-Profit Proprietary Limited (Pty Ltd) Company

A standard for-profit company, registered under the Corporations Act 2001 , offers a traditional, well-understood structure.

- Pros: This model provides simplicity in setup (relative to a co-operative), clear lines of ownership and control, and a straightforward mechanism for profit distribution to shareholders.
- Cons: A standard proprietary company is less private than other structures and can be more complex and costly to establish and operate than a sole tradership. Directors can be held personally liable for debts if they fail to meet legal obligations. From a strategic and community-engagement perspective, this structure may be perceived by NSI businesses as purely extractive, creating a standard "vendor-client" dynamic.

## 1.1.2 Option 2: Distributing Co-operative

A co-operative, registered under Queensland's Co-operatives National Law Act 2020 , is a legal entity that belongs to, is controlled by, and operates for the benefit of its members.

- Pros: This model is exceptionally aligned with the goal of partnering with the Chamber of Commerce. The Chamber's member businesses-the local SMEs-could be the co-operative's founding members. This structure:
1. Embeds Partnership: It structurally embeds the partnership, turning clients into part-owners.
2. Creates a Captive Market: Aligns incentives for the Chamber members to utilise the co-operative's services, as they share in its success.
3. Community Benefit: A distributing co-operative is permitted to distribute profits to its members , ensuring value is retained within the NSI business community.
4. Democratic Control: It operates on a strict "1 member, 1 vote" system, regardless of shareholding size.
- Cons: Co-operatives are more regulated than other structures and democratic decision-making can be slower, as it requires consensus among members.

## 1.1.3 Recommendation for Consultation

The strongest strategic approach is to propose the Distributing Co-operative as the primary model for consultation with the Straddie Chamber of Commerce. It transforms the business narrative from "outsourcing" to "collective empowerment," which directly aligns with the Chamber's own mission to "represent, advocate for, and empower the diverse business community".

If this model is chosen, 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' could adhere to the specific registration requirements in Queensland :

1. Minimum Membership: A co-operative (other than a co-operative group) could have at least five members.
2. Formation Meeting: A formal formation meeting could be held to adopt the rules and present the disclosure statement.
3. Approved Rules: The co-operative could draft and submit its proposed rules to the Registrar for approval before the formation meeting.
4. Disclosure Statement: As a distributing co-operative, RSC could prepare a draft disclosure statement outlining members' financial rights and responsibilities and have it approved by the Registrar.

The relevant forms for this process include Form 1 (Application for approval of draft formation disclosure statement), Form 2 (Application for approval of proposed rules), and Form 3 (Application for registration of a co-operative).

## 2.0 The Regulatory Engine: Licensing and 'Overcompliance' Framework

This section details the non-negotiable legal licenses required to operate the 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' model. It identifies a major legal barrier in the plan for skilled migration and provides the specific, complex solution required to overcome it.

## 2.1 Mandatory Queensland Labour Hire Provider Licensing

The Tier 3 (Labour Hire) service model is governed by a strict state-level licensing scheme.

- The Law: The Labour Hire Licensing Act 2017 (QLD) establishes a mandatory licensing scheme for all labour hire providers operating in Queensland. The definition is broad: a business (RSC) that supplies a worker (employee) to another person (the NSI business) to do work.
- Obligations: 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' could apply for and obtain this license before supplying any staff. The application process requires the business to:
1. Pass the 'Fit and Proper Person' test to establish they are capable of providing services in compliance with all relevant laws.
2. Prove the business is 'Financially Viable'.
- Operational Requirements: Once licensed, RSC could provide six-monthly reports on all labour hire activities, including compliance with workplace laws.
- Client-Side Risk (The "Hook"): Critically, it is illegal for a client (Chamber members) to engage an unlicensed labour hire provider. The penalties for doing so are severe, exceeding $500,000 for a corporation. This regulation forms a key part of RSC's "de-risking" value proposition; engaging RSC is the only way for a local business to compliantly source labour-hire staff.

## 2.2 The Important Path: Standard Business Sponsorship (SBS) vs. The On-Hire Labour Agreement (OHLA)

A core component of the RSC model is the plan to sponsor skilled migrants (e.g., chefs, tourism managers) and supply them to NSI businesses. This plan faces a significant legal barrier under standard visa programs.

- The Blocker: Standard Business Sponsorship (SBS) A standard business sponsorship (SBS) allows an approved business to sponsor a skilled worker for itself, typically under the Subclass 482 (Temporary Skill Shortage) visa. However, the sponsorship obligations tied to the SBS framework make it unsuitable for RSC's model. Specifically, Standard
- Business Sponsorship obligation 3A prohibits an employer from supplying a sponsored worker to another business unless that business is an "associated entity" (e.g., part of the same corporate group).This means the standard SBS model legally forbids 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' from sponsoring a chef and then supplying that chef to work at a client's hotel.
- The Solution: The On-Hire Labour Agreement (OHLA) The only viable pathway is for 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' to negotiate a specific On-Hire Labour Agreement (OHLA) with the Department of Home Affairs.An OHLA is a special type of labour agreement that specifically permits an approved sponsor (RSC) to sponsor workers on a Subclass 482 (Labour Agreement Stream) visa and then "on-hire" them to unrelated third-party clients (the NSI businesses). Under this arrangement, 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' remains the legal employer and sponsor, responsible for all sponsorship obligations.
- OHLA Application Requirements and Strategic Implications Negotiating an OHLA is a complex, high-stakes process with significant prerequisites.
1. Could be an Existing Business: The business could be lawfully operating in Australia for at least 12 months.
2. Labour Market Testing (LMT): Could provide extensive evidence of genuine efforts to recruit Australian workers first.
3. Stakeholder Consultation: The business could consult with relevant industry stakeholders (e.g., unions) before lodging the request.
4. Submission of Contracts: The application could be accompanied by copies of the employment contract (between RSC and the worker) and the on-hire contract (between RSC and the client).
5. Processing Time: Visa applications under a Labour Agreement stream can take up to 6 months to process , and this is after the lengthy negotiation to secure the OHLA itself.

This framework dictates a fundamental re-sequencing of the Phase 1 roadmap. 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' cannot launch its skilled migration service from day one. It could operate for at least 12 months, building its domestic labour hire and CaaS/HR business, before it can even begin the OHLA application process.

## 2.3 The 'Overcompliance' Guidance: Fair Work, WHS, and Visa Tracking

The business model, particularly at Tier 3, places 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' in a high-liability position. The software platform and operational processes are therefore designed as a legal defence system.

- Shared WHS Responsibility: As a labour hire provider, RSC is a Person Conducting a Business or Undertaking (PCBU) and shares the primary duty of care for worker health and safety with the host PCBU (the client). This "contested responsibility" is a primary source of risk, as labour hire workers are often over-represented in OHS incidents.
- Accessorial Liability: Under the Fair Work Act , businesses can be held legally responsible for breaches in their supply chain, including wage theft by a contractor. This liability extends to RSC and its clients.
- Operational Requirements: The 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' platform (specified in Part 3.0) is not optional; it is the primary legal defence. It is designed for "overcompliance" (per the user query) by:
1. Automating Fair Work Act Compliance: The platform's automated award interpretation engine provides an auditable, systemic defence against underpayment claims, which are a criminal offence from 1 January 2025.
2. Automating Visa Compliance: The platform could automate visa status and work rights tracking via integration with the Visa Entitlement Verification Online (VEVO) system.
3. Automating WHS Compliance: The platform could provide a robust WHS management system for tracking incidents, risks, and worker consultation to prove due diligence has been met by both RSC and the client.

## 3.5 Vendor Analysis and Build-vs-Buy Recommendation

- Option 1: Buy (License Commercial Software)
- Analysis: Several Australian-based, all-in-one platforms exist that meet many of these requirements. Microkeeper is a strong candidate as it combines payroll, automated award interpretation, and VEVO checks. Sentrient is also a strong contender, with a heavy focus on LMS/WHS compliance modules and integrated payroll.

- Cons: Monthly licensing fees; may lack the specific flexibility needed for future integration with the Sovereign Space Builder (Phase 2); reliance on a third-party vendor.
- Option 2: Build (Custom Development)
- Analysis: This involves building the entire platform from scratch as proprietary IP.
- Pros: Creates a core intellectual property asset; complete control over features and future integration; no licensing fees; directly enables the Indigenous Developer Pathway (Part 5.3.1).
- Cons: Significantly higher upfront cost and risk; a much longer time to market (12-24 months); the technical and legal challenge of building and maintaining an Award Interpretation engine is extremely high.
- Recommendation: A Hybrid "Buy-to-Build" Strategy A hybrid approach is recommended to de-risk the launch while achieving the long-term strategic goals.
1. Phase 1 (Launch): License a "white-label" version of an existing, all-in-one Australian platform (e.g., Microkeeper or Sentrient). This achieves maximum speed-to-market and provides an immediate, legally-compliant platform to service

the first clients and generate revenue.

3. Phase 2 (Transition): Once the proprietary platform is built, stable, and certified, all clients are migrated from the white-label solution. ## 7.0 The Long-Term Vision: Monitoring the 'Braided Economy' and 'C-Hour'

This final section details the strategy for integrating the 'C-Hour' (Community-Hour) concept, which acts as the unifying data layer for the entire ecosystem. This plan is contingent on the outcomes of a key federal inquiry.

## 7.1 Defining the 'C-Hour' as a 'Regenerative Asset'

As defined in the 'Local Government Funding Inquiry Submission.pdf' (per user query), the 'C-Hour' is a new, measurable unit of non-financial value.

- Concept: The 'C-Hour' represents one hour of verified human activity dedicated to community or ecological regeneration.
- Examples: 1 C-Hour could be equivalent to 1 hour of volunteering for a QYAC ranger program (e.g., weed removal ), 1 hour of structured beach clean-up, or 1 hour of elder care.
- Regenerative Asset: The concept transforms an intangible "goodwill" activity, which is currently invisible to the market, into a quantifiable, auditable, and ultimately bankable "asset". It allows for the measurement and valuation of regenerative activities.

## 7.2 Monitoring and Integration Plan

The 'C-Hour' moves from concept to reality by leveraging a key political and economic window of opportunity.

- The Catalyst: The "House of Representatives Standing Committee on Regional Development, Infrastructure and Transport... inquiry into the funding and financial sustainability of local governments".
- Key Date to Monitor: The submission deadline for this inquiry is 3 February 2026 . * Action Plan:
1. Monitor (Feb-Dec 2026): Actively monitor the inquiry's public hearings and, most importantly, its final report and recommendations (expected mid-2026 ).
2. Align (Post-Report): The inquiry's final report will provide a new national language and framework for local government funding. The 'C-Hour' could be aligned with this. The inquiry is specifically examining "funding mechanisms," "evolving responsibilities of local governments," and "opportunities to improve productivity".
3. Positioning: The 'C-Hour' will be pitched to local and state government as a tangible solution to the problems identified in the inquiry's report-a novel,

measurable "funding mechanism" that directly supports local government in managing its "evolving responsibilities" (e.g., environmental stewardship, community wellbeing).

## 7.3 The Integration Strategy (The 'Braided Economy')

The 'C-Hour' becomes the currency of a "Braided Economy."

- Definition: A "Braided" funding model uses multiple funding sources to support a single project, while tracking those funds individually to meet their separate compliance requirements.
- Monetisation Strategy: The 'C-Hour' will be monetised by creating a "C-Hour Fund." This fund will be filled with "braided" capital from:
- Federal/State environmental grants (e.g., for dune regeneration).
- Regenerative tourism and eco-cultural funds (linking to QYAC's strategy ).
- Corporate ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) funds.
- Philanthropic grants.
- Platform Integration: The entire ecosystem is designed to manage this:
- Phase 2 ('Sovereign Space Builder'): This app is the technical platform and "digital wallet" for the C-Hour.
- ■ Function 1 (Issue): QYAC and local non-profits can issue C-Hour "bounties" (e.g., "100 C-Hours needed for dune restoration at Home Beach").
- ■ Function 2 (Track): Locals and tourists use the app to log and verify their C-Hours (e.g., via GPS check-in and photo verification).
- ■ Function 3 (Redeem): The C-Hour Fund (from braided grants) pays participants for their C-Hours, or C-Hours can be redeemed for discounts at participating NSI businesses (e.g., 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' clients), creating a truly circular, regenerative economy.
- Phase 3 ('Quandamooka Intelligence'): This entity is the analytical engine . Its role is to model the C-Hour data, proving its ecological and social impact to the grant providers. This "impact report" is what ensures the "braided" funding is renewed, making the system self-sustaining.

The 'C-Hour' is thus the unifying data layer that connects all four phases of the ecosystem-from the (Phase 1) business owner who accepts it, to the (Phase 2) app that tracks it, to the (Phase 3) AI that models it, to the (Phase 4) wellness programs that use it as a metric for a healthy life.

## 8.0 Concluding Strategic Summary

This report has detailed an integrated, eight-part strategy to launch a multi-phase economic ecosystem on Minjerribah.

The analysis confirms that the venture's success hinges on a series of important dependencies and a precisely-phased execution:

1. The 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' (RSC) entity is the commercial engine. Its viability is confirmed by the clear, high-stakes compliance "pain points" of the ~180 SMEs on NSI. The "Distributing Co-operative" model is identified as the superior legal structure for achieving the required community-embedded partnership with the Straddie Chamber of Commerce.
2. The regulatory framework is the primary risk and barrier to entry. The plan for skilled
3. migration is not viable with a Standard Business Sponsorship. The venture could pursue the complex, high-barrier On-Hire Labour Agreement (OHLA) , which requires a 12-month operational history. This dictates a mandatory re-sequencing of the Phase 1 launch.
3. The software platform is the central legal defence. The "overcompliance" architecture, integrating HRIS, LMS, and WHS, is non-negotiable. The "Buy-to-Build" hybrid strategy is the recommended path, allowing RSC to generate revenue from a white-label platform while its own Indigenous-led developer team builds the proprietary, long-term asset.
4. The Quandamooka Intelligence (QI) entity is the human capital goal. The 16-week, paid "Quandamooka Developer Bootcamp" is the mechanism to create this entity, modeled on successful programs and hosted at QYAC's Minjerribah Ganaba facility.
5. The QYAC partnership is the "social license." Engagement could be phased, patient, and aligned with QYAC's 2022-2026 Strategic Plan. The precedent set by the QYAC-UQ MOU provides the template for a future Joint Venture.
6. The 'C-Hour' is the unifying data layer. The entire ecosystem's long-term vision culminates in the creation of a 'Braided Economy'. The 2026 Local Government Funding Inquiry is the external trigger to launch this, using the Sovereign Space Builder as the wallet and QI as the analytical engine.

In synthesis, 'Ready S.E.T. Co.' is not merely an HR company; it is the "Trojan Horse" for a far larger vision. It is a profitable, commercially-sound B2B service that acts as the incubator, funding engine, and regulatory sandbox for the entire ecosystem. Its success in Phase 1 provides the revenue, talent, and trust required to execute all subsequent phases.
