Civic AI and shared capability

Understand a local question, learn a useful skill and help your community operate the tools it depends on.

Adapted from the 23 September 2026 plan. The proposed system is open to development with participants.

Begin with one local question

Civic AI is artificial intelligence used to help people understand and participate in matters affecting their community. A useful first task might be finding the source of a council proposal, explaining a public document in ordinary language or comparing options for a local project. Exploring sources and questioning explanations would be part of the learning.

The Purple Party for Australia or P4A materials explore civic learning, simulation, humour and participation in Australia, Oceania and Native Nations contexts. Begin with one local question, read the underlying material and compare more than one interpretation. Civic participation in the C-hour programme is open to people with different political views. Joining does not require support for any existing or hypothetical party or a constitutional proposal.

Learn while doing useful work

An AI surge brings learning, tools and practical projects together. Community members can help one another use AI, build local reference collections, check generated material and develop useful services. C-hours recognise voluntary teaching and checking. Specialist employment, equipment and operating costs need separate funding via the traditional market economy.

Available devices and a shared task offer a practical starting point for local infrastructure. The cultural intelligence node, community kiosks and larger computing proposals suggest different possibilities as demand grows. Hosts, participants and builders would explore their usefulness, cost, privacy and maintenance together. A public account of what is running, being tried and still imagined would help others find where to contribute.

A doorway in everyday places

Digital notice boards offer a public doorway into the experiment. A willing host and a few real invitations would give people somewhere to begin. An agent conversation, a host’s help or a private session on a shared device are possible routes. Trying these with people who have different devices and access needs would shape the experience.

Keep useful knowledge close to home

Continuity matters too. Local copies of essential directories, project records and relevant maps can help when connectivity is poor. Realistic trials of power, support, backups and restoration would reveal where the local setup needs attention. Proposed links with clean energy and useful recovery of computing heat need engineering and operating plans suited to each site.

The long-term opportunity is a community that can use powerful tools while retaining the knowledge and relationships needed to operate them. People learn through useful work, and the infrastructure grows around demonstrated needs.
